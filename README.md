# Engagement & Incentives

## Enrollment ≠ activation

Enrollment tells you someone has access to a product or program.

It doesn't tell you whether they've experienced enough value to become an active user.

That creates a different product question:

> **How do you design an incentive around the behavior you want to change rather than starting with an offer and measuring who interacted with it?**

---

## The scenario

Consider a fictional loyalty program with **100,000 enrolled members**.

Only **58% have completed a qualifying action** since joining.

The remaining enrolled members aren't necessarily disengaged. Some may simply never have crossed the point where membership became useful to them.

For this product, activation is defined as:

> **Completing a first qualifying purchase after enrollment.**

The objective isn't to get members to open an offer.

It's to increase the number who reach that first meaningful product outcome.

---

## Start with the behavior

A tempting starting point is:

> Send inactive members a 2× points offer.

But that begins with the solution.

Instead, define the behavioral objective first.

**Objective:** Increase first qualifying purchases among enrolled-but-not-activated members.

From there, the rest of the campaign can be designed around that behavior.

| Decision | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Audience | Recently enrolled members who haven't activated |
| Eligibility | Enrolled + no qualifying purchase + contactable + promotion eligible |
| Incentive | 2× points on first qualifying purchase |
| Success event | First qualifying purchase |
| Measurement window | 14 days after exposure |

The incentive is one product choice inside the strategy. It isn't the strategy itself.

---

## The product decision

**Start with the behavior, then design the incentive.**

The product should be able to answer:

**Who are we trying to move?**  
Enrolled members who haven't activated.

**What behavior are we trying to create?**  
A first qualifying purchase.

**Why might an incentive help?**  
It increases the immediate value of completing that action.

**What counts as success?**  
The member completes the qualifying behavior within the measurement window.

This keeps audience definition, eligibility, incentive design, and measurement tied to the same objective.

---

## Enrollment, activation & engagement

These states describe different relationships with the product.

**Enrolled → Activated → Engaged → Retained**

### Enrolled

Has access to the program.

### Activated

Has completed the first behavior that demonstrates initial product value.

### Engaged

Continues completing meaningful behaviors over time.

### Retained

Continues returning and receiving value over a longer period.

Treating these as the same thing can make a large membership number look healthier than the underlying product behavior actually is.

It can also lead to incentives being sent without a clear understanding of which lifecycle transition they're intended to influence.

---

## Targeting the intervention

For this activation campaign, the target isn't simply "members who haven't purchased."

The audience should represent the lifecycle problem we're actually trying to solve.

**Enrolled within the last 30 days**  
↓  
**No qualifying purchase**  
↓  
**Contactable**  
↓  
**Eligible for the promotion**  
↓  
**Activation audience**

Each rule has a purpose.

The enrollment window keeps the intervention tied to activation rather than long-term re-engagement.

The behavioral filter identifies members who haven't reached the activation event.

Contact and promotion eligibility prevent the campaign from targeting people who cannot or should not receive it.

---

## Offer performance ≠ behavior change

An offer can perform well without changing much behavior.

Metrics such as:

- opens
- clicks
- offer views
- claims

tell us whether people interacted with the campaign.

They don't tell us whether the campaign caused more people to activate.

The success event for this case is the **first qualifying purchase**.

But even the number of members who activate after receiving the offer isn't enough on its own.

Some of them would have activated anyway.

---

## Measuring incrementality

The campaign should be evaluated against what would likely have happened without the intervention.

Split the eligible audience into:

**Treatment**  
Receives the 2× points activation incentive.

**Control**  
Receives the normal program experience.

Suppose the result is:

| Group | Activation rate |
| --- | ---: |
| Treatment | 24% |
| Control | 18% |

The treatment group activated at a higher rate, but the meaningful result isn't simply:

**24% activated.**

It's:

**+6 percentage-point incremental lift**

The control group provides evidence that some activation would have happened without the incentive.

That distinction matters when deciding whether the incentive actually changed behavior and whether its cost was justified.

## Measurement

### Primary

**Incremental activation lift**

The difference in activation between the treatment and control groups during the defined measurement window.

### Supporting measures

- **Activation rate** — what percentage of eligible members completed the first qualifying action?
- **Time to first qualifying purchase** — how quickly are members reaching activation after enrollment or exposure?
- **Incentive use rate** — how many targeted members completed a qualifying purchase while eligible for the incentive?
- **Progression to subsequent engagement** — do members who activate continue into meaningful repeat behavior?

### Guardrails

- **Incentive cost** — how much additional reward value was required to generate the incremental behavior?
- **Incremental margin / contribution** — did the additional behavior generate enough economic value to justify the incentive cost?
- **Cannibalization** — how much incentive value went toward behavior that likely would have happened without the promotion?
- **Promotion opt-outs** — is targeting or promotional frequency causing members to opt out?
- **Over-targeting or repeated exposure** — are members becoming dependent on incentives before acting?

A campaign that increases activation isn't automatically successful. The incremental behavior has to justify the cost of creating it.

---

## What I'd validate

The campaign design starts with a hypothesis: increasing the immediate value of the first qualifying action will move more enrolled members into activation.

I'd validate:

- Is first qualifying purchase actually a meaningful activation event?
- Where do newly enrolled members currently drop off before activation?
- Does the incentive create incremental behavior or mostly subsidize existing intent?
- Which members respond differently to the intervention?
- Does activation driven by the incentive lead to subsequent engagement?
- What incentive cost produces enough incremental value to justify the intervention?

The goal isn't to optimize an offer in isolation.

It's to understand whether the intervention creates **meaningful behavior change**.
